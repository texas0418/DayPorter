Why the wage is never the cost

Ask a cleaning contractor what labor costs them and most will say the wage. It is the number they negotiated, the number on the offer letter, and the number they carry in their head when a customer asks for a price.

It is also wrong by 25–45%, every time.

The moment someone is on payroll you owe employer-side payroll taxes — 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare, plus federal and state unemployment. You owe workers' compensation, and janitorial classification codes are not cheap, because this trade involves wet floors, chemicals and ladders. You carry general liability. If you offer benefits, uniforms or a phone allowance, those are real dollars per hour too.

Then there is the part almost nobody prices: paid hours nobody works. Six holidays and five PTO days is 88 paid hours a year with no one in the building. You still owe that money, and the only place it can come from is the hours that are worked — so your true rate per worked hour is higher again.

None of this is optional or unusual. It is simply what employing people costs, and the contractors who quote off the raw wage are not cheaper than their competitors — they are just absorbing the difference out of their own margin, usually without realising it.

Common questions

Is 2,080 hours the right base?

It is the standard full-time year — 40 hours across 52 weeks. This calculator uses it to work out what share of paid hours are actually productive. Part-time staff have the same rate; only the annual totals change.

What if I use subcontractors instead of employees?

Then payroll taxes and workers' comp are the subcontractor's problem, and their invoice rate is closer to your true cost. Verify they genuinely carry their own comp and liability, and understand that misclassifying employees as contractors is an expensive mistake in this industry.

Should overtime be in here?

Not in the base rate. Price the standard scope at the loaded rate, then handle overtime as its own cost when a job requires it. Burying expected overtime in the base rate hides which accounts are actually causing it.

Does this replace advice from an accountant?

No. It is a straightforward arithmetic model to get you a defensible working number. Your accountant and your insurance broker have the exact figures for your business.
