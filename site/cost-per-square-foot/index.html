Why "cost per square foot" is the wrong question

It is the most-searched number in commercial cleaning and the least useful one, because it is an output, not an input. Nobody prices a building by looking up a rate per square foot. They work out how many labor hours the space takes, multiply by what labor costs, add supplies and margin — and cost per square foot is simply that answer divided by the floor area.

Which means every published benchmark is quietly assuming three things it usually does not tell you:

Frequency. Five nights a week costs about five times what one night a week costs, per square foot. A benchmark that does not state visits per week is not a benchmark.

Production rate. An open warehouse might clean at 8,000 sq ft an hour. A restaurant with a kitchen and two restrooms might manage 1,800. That is a 4× spread in labor before anything else is considered.

Labor market. The same scope in a market where loaded labor is $27 an hour costs 35% more than where it is $20. No national average survives that.

So use cost per square foot the way it is actually useful: as a sanity check on a number you built from labor. If your price per square foot is far below what your own production and labor rates support, you have not found an efficiency — you have made an arithmetic error, and the account will underperform for as long as you hold it.

Common questions

My price is well below the benchmark. Is that bad?

Usually, yes — but check the inputs first. If your crews genuinely clean faster than the production rate you selected, the benchmark is wrong for you and your price may be fine. If they do not, the account is running on a thinner margin than you think, or on none at all. The contract profitability check settles it with real hours.

My price is well above it. Should I worry about losing the account?

Not automatically. Higher-frequency scopes, day porter coverage, consumables supply and demanding specifications all legitimately raise the number. What matters is being able to explain the difference — a customer who understands what they are buying rarely leaves over price alone.

Should I quote customers a price per square foot?

Generally no. It invites a comparison against a number they found online that assumes a different frequency and a different scope. Quote a monthly price against a written scope, and keep cost per square foot as your own internal check.

Does this include periodic work like stripping and waxing?

No, and it should not. Floor refinishing, carpet extraction and window cleaning are priced as separate line items. Folding them into a recurring per-square-foot rate makes the account look expensive and the periodic work look free.
